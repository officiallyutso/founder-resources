**Where extending the EU's carbon border adjustment mechanism does, and does not, make sense**

Source: Elise Viadere, Centre for European Reform. Original date: 16 November 2026. Reading time: about 16 minutes.

Direct URL: https://www.cer.eu/insights/where-extending-eus-carbon-border-adjustment-mechanism-make-sense

What happened. Viadere writes that negotiators from the Council, the European Parliament and the Commission meet again on 30 November to decide how far CBAM should extend from basic steel and aluminium into finished goods, with a proposed start in 2028. She compares three lists. The Commission proposed around 180 downstream products in December 2025. The Council proposed a broader list, with around 200 additional entries, in June 2026. Parliament proposed around 450 products in September. She converts the lists into one customs classification and gets 386 products, of which 378 have enough data to score.

Her finding, on her own thresholds: 103 of the 378 products have both a low estimated carbon cost and no concentrated import dependence. Nearly 70 percent of Parliament's additions, by import value, would face a carbon charge below 1 percent of price. At the other end, 33 products clear both of her tests. Refrigerators and metal furniture, on her estimate, face carbon costs of about 3 to 4 percent of import price. Static converters (power supplies, chargers, inverters) are her warning case: EU imports of €14.7 billion a year, two-thirds from China, and an estimated CBAM charge of 0.36 percent of price.

The method note is the line a forge should read twice. She multiplies metal content by 1.9 tonnes of CO2 per tonne of steel, "roughly the global average," or 1.55 tonnes per tonne of aluminium, and by a carbon price of €82.32 a tonne. Articles of iron or steel, such as nails and chains, are counted as all metal. Other goods, such as refrigerators and pumps, are assumed to be 79 percent steel or aluminium by weight, "the average for the Commission's list." She writes that these are estimates of a potential charge, "not a current CBAM bill," and that the calculation "does not use plant-level or country-specific emissions."

Her circumvention example is physical, not rhetorical. Turning steel wire into nails before import could step outside today's scope while leaving the steel's emissions largely unchanged. She also states that a Russian complaint produced a WTO dispute panel against the existing CBAM in September 2026, and that in July 2026 the Commission proposed pushing the end of free ETS permits for CBAM sectors from 2034 to 2038. Those two sentences are hers. They were not re-checked against a WTO docket or the July proposal text.

Evidence quality: independently reported analysis. She cites the Commission proposal COM(2025) 989, Council document ST 10423/26, Parliament amendments P10_TA(2026)0276, and Eurostat Comext and Prodcom. It is not a law, not a peer-reviewed paper, and not a measurement from a mill or a forge. The 1 percent and one-third lines are her cut-offs. The 30 November date is her statement of the meeting. This edition did not find an EU institution calendar page that lists the day.

Why it matters to Stamped. A European buyer of fasteners, components or machinery will be asked for the steel in the part and for a carbon figure attached to it. The figure in this chart is a world average of 1.9 tonnes per tonne. The forge's own route, the heat, the scrap, and the furnace, is the document that average is standing in for. The link is direct for a plant that ships into EU supply chains. It is weak for a forge that sells only inside India. Nothing here is a Stamped result, and the chart is not a bill.

Post thesis. The argument on 30 November is about which finished good inherits the steel's carbon. The number being used to sort the list is a global average, and the author says so. A buyer who accepts that average has not yet asked which heat made the part.

Who to tag, and why. Tag the Centre for European Reform only after the handle is checked on the day. An X search for Elise Viadere returned other people with similar names, and none of those accounts is hers. No personal handle is invented here. A second tag is worth it only for a forging metallurgist who will ask what 1.9 tonnes per tonne does to an Indian induction route versus a blast furnace. No such handle was verified for this edition.

Limitations. The lists are negotiating positions. A product on Parliament's list is not in force. Her 79 percent assumption will mis-state a machined forging, which is closer to her "all metal" case (nails, chains) than to a refrigerator. The €82.32 price is an input, not a forecast she defends. The 30 November meeting could move. Free-permit timing and the WTO panel are reported inside the essay, not re-audited here.

### Section 3: General learning

Three free pieces, opened, from three publications. None of these articles are in the 14-day log. Quanta, Works in Progress and Asterisk have been used before for other essays. These three are different pieces.

**1. Science isn't a series of lucky accidents**
Saloni Dattani, Works in Progress, 16 November 2026. About 7 minutes. https://worksinprogress.co/issue/accidental-discoveries/

The essay page prints the date and not a byline in the body. The magazine front attributes the column to Saloni Dattani: https://worksinprogress.co/

She takes three dinner-party discoveries apart. Fleming's open-window penicillin story first appears in a 1945 interview, sixteen years after the 1929 paper, and colleagues said the window was seldom opened. Ronald Hare's reconstruction, which she checked against his notes at the Wellcome Library, puts the spores in a lab one floor below, on a plate Fleming forgot to incubate, during a cold spell. Pasteur's chicken-cholera vaccine, on the notebooks his grandson later released, was run by Émile Roux while Pasteur was at his daughter's wedding. Röntgen's glowing-screen story is a later hypothesis: his notes were burned, and he was in a long fight with Philipp Lenard over credit. Her sharper point is motive. Geison, a historian she cites, suggests Pasteur kept the attenuation method quiet because it was simple enough for competitors to copy, and French patent law did not cover medicines. She quotes Niko McCarty on "engineered serendipity": the optogenetics inventors wrote the requirements down, then listed every way they could imagine to meet them.

Utso's takeaway: a plant story that begins with "we just noticed the scrap fell" is usually a notebook that nobody filed. The useful version is the one that says who changed the soak, and when.

Mental model: chance is what the write-up says after the record is gone. Write the requirement down before the trial.

**2. What Makes Up the Cosmos? A 20-Year Survey Hints That Something Is Missing.**
Charlie Wood, Quanta Magazine, 16 November 2026. A correction on the page is dated 17 November 2026 and does not change the statistical claim below. About 16 minutes. https://www.quantamagazine.org/what-makes-up-the-cosmos-a-20-year-survey-hints-that-something-is-missing-20261116/

The Dark Energy Survey's final analysis, which Wood reports as published in early 2026 from data taken on 758 nights between 2013 and 2019, fits a weakening dark energy somewhat better than the standard Lambda-CDM picture. DES reports a three-sigma preference. Wood is explicit that three sigma is enough to pay attention to, and short of the five sigma physicists require before calling a discovery. He also reports that the mismatch is not fixed by throwing out one dataset: the cosmic microwave background, the matter maps, and the supernovas disagree with each other in pairs. Simone Ferraro is quoted: there appear to be cracks, and "there's something that doesn't fit." A 2025 analysis Ferraro and collaborators did, as Wood reports it, can improve the fit if an early-universe date moves by about 200 million years. That alternative is in the piece so the three-sigma result is not the only story.

Utso's takeaway: a drift flag at "interesting" is a reason to pull the furnace chart. It is not a reason to rewrite the process standard. Five sigma, in a plant, is closer to a repeat on the next heat.

Mental model: three sigma is a reason to look. Five sigma is a reason to change the setting.

**3. We are all playable characters**
Hannah Kim, assistant professor of philosophy at the University of Arizona. Substack publication of the Asterisk essay, 12 November 2026. The page says it first appeared in Asterisk, Issue 16, Flourishing. About 15 minutes. https://asteriskmag.substack.com/p/we-are-all-playable-characters

Kim's argument is that a life-as-story leaves the meaning of an event open until the ending, and that games supply the missing piece: a next move, with a score, while you are still inside the hour. She uses C. Thi Nguyen: a game's medium is agency, because the player takes up goals they do not otherwise hold. Character types (her examples run from Theophrastus to "girlboss") compress a person into a role that already knows what counts as playing it well. She calls the efficiency a risk. Nguyen's phrase in her piece is "value capture": the game, or the role, defines success so completely that you stop writing your own. The essay is cultural, not industrial. The transferable piece is the scoreboard.

Utso's takeaway: a shift that is handed a role ("hit the numbers") will play the number it was given. If the number is tonnes out the door, scrap is someone else's board.

Mental model: people play the score that is on the wall. Change the score, and the next move changes with it.
