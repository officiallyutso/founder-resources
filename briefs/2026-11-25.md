**Why it matters.** A die caster and a secondary smelter buy scrap as charge. A primary smelter meets that scrap as a rival to its ingot. The 24 November note tries to settle the rivalry with a chemistry screen (above 91 percent aluminium) and a duty that waits on that screen. The 12 November account was a grade-and-duty screen, with no 91 percent figure. A plant that prices November metal off either letter is pricing a draft. The spectrometer print on yesterday's lot is the number that already exists.

**Inference, labelled as such.** A floor written as "percent aluminium" does not name iron, copper or zinc. Those are the tramp elements that sludge and blister a high-pressure die-cast melt. A lot can clear 91 percent aluminium and still be a bad charge. That sentence is not in the AAI note.

**Testable consequence.** Until a gazette, the charge mix does not change. A plant can already sort recent imported lots by spectrometer aluminium, iron, copper and zinc, and count how many would fail a 91 percent screen. That count is the size of the letter. I do not know what share of any one NCR die caster's charge is imported scrap. That share is Unknown.

**Post thesis.** Primary producers have asked for imported scrap above 91 percent aluminium, with the duty held or raised until that rule exists. Twelve days earlier the recyclers' public ask was for the scrap duty to go. A die caster's spectrometer has received neither letter. The purity floor still does not name the iron in the melt.

## Section 3. General learning

Three free pieces, three topics. None of them is Quanta, Asterisk, Works in Progress, or the other outlets used on 20 and 23 November.

**1. Manufacture vs. Construct.** Liv Jenks, Union Square Ventures, 20 November 2026. About 8 minutes. https://www.usv.com/writing/manufacture-vs-construct

Construction makes one thing, in place, once. Manufacturing makes the same thing many times, under conditions you can hold still, and the cost falls as the count rises. Jenks' point is that a lot of "projects" (a data centre, a reactor, a robot cell) become products when you freeze the unit and let the data from each unit teach the next. The line that travels: a construction business grows by adding crews, and a product moves down a cost curve because each round of learning cuts waste.

**Carry forward.** A forge already lives on the product side of that line. The learning only compounds if the same defect is recorded the same way on the next batch. A one-off customer complaint is a construction project. A defect tied to a heat is a manufacturing record.

**2. Thinking is Hard (for Humans).** Jacob Falkovich, LessWrong, 23 November 2026. About 15 minutes. https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/NyCFKxR74RgRbs7SM/thinking-is-hard-for-humans

Falkovich's distinction: the fast system follows the single most salient cue and drops the rest of the context. The slow system is the one that can hold a few chunks in working memory and keep the original intention in view. He is writing about people handing judgment to models that now run longer than a person's attention. The useful bit is older than the models. When the stakes are high, the salient detail (a headline number, a single bad part) crowds out the rest of the picture.

**Carry forward.** A record quarter, a single scrap percentage, or a single duty proposal is a salient cue. The slow question is which other number has to sit beside it before you change a setting.

**3. What's Missing from the American Industrial Revival?** Kyle Murphy and Jake Higdon, NEIS Center, 20 November 2026. About 12 minutes. https://neiscenter.substack.com/p/whats-missing-from-the-american-industrial

Their bottleneck is information, in both directions. States cannot see the national priority. The capital cannot see what a region can actually make. They report Clean Investment Monitor's second-quarter figure: US clean-tech factory investment up 4 percent to $8 billion, still down 24 percent year on year. They also report a participant's regional poll (Indiana, Ohio, Pennsylvania, Michigan): more than 80 percent calling heavy industry critically or very important, and about 72 percent willing to pay more for cleaner production. A comment on the piece, from Zack Valdez, argues that a new federal institution matters only if someone can line up suppliers, workforce money and purchasing on a timeline a factory can plan against. The poll is one participant's, and the $8 billion is the monitor's.

**Carry forward.** A national scheme and a plant ledger answer different questions. Asking one to stand in for the other is how a carbon intensity, a gas price, or a scrap duty becomes a number nobody on the floor can audit.
