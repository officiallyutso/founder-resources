**1. Härtel, Alimov, Jiang and Gardill, “From process signals to design decisions,” Production Engineering, 18 November 2026.** Open access. https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11740-026-01483-x

On an SMS screw press forging an EN AW-6060 part, random-forest importance ranks billet length, billet diameter and the billet’s offset in the y-direction as the main drivers of underfill. SHAP windows from the simulation set: length beyond about 72 mm and diameter above about 34.5 mm sit with lower underfill contribution; the y-offset window that stays defect-free is about −5 mm to +5 mm. Press-frame stiffness barely moves defect probability inside the range they tried, with a slight rise only below 6 MN/mm, and ram tilt matters at about −0.075°. A 120 GHz radar separates a centred billet from one shifted +10 mm or −10 mm, and those shifts were the underfilled trials. Evidence: peer-reviewed, open access, one press and one aluminium part, 600 finite-element runs for the main set. A production hammer in Faridabad is not this press. The useful tension is the ranking: geometry and placement outrank the soft frame they bothered to model.

## Section 3. General learning

Three free pieces, three sources, none from the outlets used on 13 to 25 November.

**1. Scott Alexander, “The AI Forecasting Agenda,” Astral Codex Ten, 19 November 2026.** About 15 minutes. https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/the-ai-forecasting-agenda

Central idea: specialised forecasting models now match top humans on ordinary questions, and foundation models are close behind. The open problems are conditional questions (“if this, then what”), causal questions (the conditional is not the cause), and questions where the model may refuse to leave a consensus. He still uses a forecast he cannot fully defend, because a number forces the disagreement into a crux: “3 points is wrong” versus “3 points is enough.”

Carry forward: when a plant number shows up (a scrap rate, a carbon factor, a power average), ask which question it actually answers. A daily average does not answer “what did the 6 pm heat cost.”

**2. Jack Clark, Import AI 482, 23 November 2026.** About 15 minutes for the issue. The section to keep is the Lawrence Livermore heat-conduction benchmark. https://jack-clark.net/2026/11/23/import-ai-482-huawei-kernel-benchmarks-airborne-drone-detection-and-a-national-labs-ai-science-benchmark/

Central idea, in the researchers’ line he quotes: an agent that reports a confident wrong number is worse than one that reports honest uncertainty, because nobody downstream can tell them apart. The benchmark makes the agent design a few heating experiments, fit a heat-conduction simulator that may be physically wrong, and return conductivity and heat capacity with a 95% interval. Only the strongest models stay reliable when the simulator is missing real physics.

Carry forward: on a furnace, a filled-in thermocouple that looks precise is the dangerous output. The useful output is the interval, plus a flag that the model of the wall does not match the wall.

**3. Gergely Orosz, “The Pulse: What can we learn from the Railway hack?”, The Pragmatic Engineer, 19 November 2026.** Free bonus issue, about 12 minutes. https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/the-pulse-what-can-we-learn-from-132

Central idea: Railway’s own timeline has the defender minutes behind an attacker that moved through stolen code in hours. Credentials used to download the repo were revoked one minute after they were used, and eight minutes after the probing started. The next steps still landed. A rival that published a “migrate off Railway” post was hit in a similar way nine days later, and took longer to notice.

Carry forward: a write-up the next morning is a different product from a tap on the shoulder during the heat. Speed of the handoff beats a complete explanation that arrives after the batch is scrap.
